## Summary for the action/regression question

Quigg distinguishes Noether's two theorems: finite-parameter continuous variational symmetries give conservation laws; symmetries depending on arbitrary functions give differential identities. His central contrast is between an observed regularity and a consequence of an assumed symmetry, not between squared errors and action. The physics discussion connects this to phase conventions, gauge interactions, and path-dependent information. He explicitly allows a proposed symmetry principle to be physically wrong. Most of the essay concerns Noether's mathematical development, exclusion from academic employment, collaborators, and exile; its postscript discusses Yang–Mills theory.

**Locations:** pp. 1–3 for the two theorems and conservation; pp. 7–9 for gauge and path dependence; p. 14 for the postscript. Nothing here establishes an anti-regression theorem. Symmetry constrains admissible physical models; observations still test those models.

## Our mathematical comparison, not a quotation from Quigg

For a differentiable mechanical Lagrangian L(q, q̇, t), stationary paths satisfy

    d/dt (∂L/∂q̇) − ∂L/∂q = 0.

If L is independent of q, then p := ∂L/∂q̇ is conserved along such a path. If L is independent of t, then E := q̇·p − L is conserved. These are elementary instances, not the full generality of either theorem. More general transformations and invariance up to boundary terms require the corresponding boundary correction to the conserved quantity.

A least-squares fit can estimate a parameter inside such an L, or fit observations to trajectories generated by it. The fit does not create the conservation law: the variational symmetry supplies that constraint. Conversely, a small residual does not establish the assumed symmetry. To compare the philosophies, ask what fixes the admissible model and what would count as evidence against it.

Do not equate stationary action with a minimum, or infer a unique interaction law from Noether's theorems alone. Those are additional mathematical/physical claims requiring their own assumptions.
